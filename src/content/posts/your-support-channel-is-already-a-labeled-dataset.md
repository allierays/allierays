The first eval harness I shipped could not fail.

It was a golden test for a search agent. Each case had a question and the tool call we expected the agent to make. The test built a mock agent that returned the expected tool call, ran the "agent," and asserted that the output matched. Green every time. It stayed green through two real regressions before anyone read it closely enough to notice that it was asserting a mock against itself.

We deleted it. The replacement doc opens with that confession, and the file that survives keeps it in the docstring, because the failure mode is common enough to deserve a warning label: a recording of what the system did is not a label. If your "expected" values were generated by the thing you are testing, you have a snapshot, not an eval.

This post is about what we built instead, across two production systems at Go1: a content search platform over a catalog of 111,000 learning items, and a pipeline intelligence layer that answers revenue questions for sellers. Both had the same problem. People used them, nobody could say whether they were right, and there was no budget for a labeling team.

## Where the labels were hiding

The thing that unblocked us was noticing that the labels already existed. We just had not been treating them as labels.

Sales and customer success reps had been asking for content in a Slack channel for a year. Each request was a question with business context: the prospect's industry, region, size, what they were trying to do. And each one got an answer from a content expert, usually a list of links. That is a query plus a graded answer set. Fifteen of those messages became our first real eval queries, verbatim, with the expert's picks turned into a list of terms the answer had to cover.

The intake form the same team used was the schema. Its fields mapped one-to-one onto the search filters, so seventeen more cases came from mirroring the form exactly. Then a customer RFP handed us a third corpus: the content categories an enterprise buyer says they need, written by the buyer.

On the revenue side it was the same shape. The golden question set started as one RevOps leader's written list of the questions she actually gets asked. Later we added six more from the first executive to drive the system cold in a live session.

None of this required annotation. It required reading the places where experts were already doing the work and writing it down in a format a script could replay.

Today the search golden set has 72 cases from three sources, 32 of them carrying relevance labels, 647 labels total on a 0 to 3 scale. The revenue side has 61 golden questions, 49 of them automated.

## Replay it, score the gap

Once you have queries and graded answers, the eval is a replay. Run every query against the live system, compare what came back to what the expert would have picked, aggregate.

The metrics are boring on purpose. Recall at 30 asks whether the right items made it into the candidate pool at all. nDCG at 10 asks whether they were ranked where a person would see them. MRR asks how far down the first good result sits. Each one is a pure function with no I/O, unit-tested against hand-computed values, and each returns nothing rather than zero when it is undefined, so a case with no labels cannot drag the average down with a fake zero.

The first baseline told us exactly where the problem was:

| Metric | Value |
|---|---|
| recall@30 | 1.00 |
| nDCG@10 | 0.78 |
| MRR | 0.69 |

Recall was perfect. Every relevant item was in the pool every time. Ranking was where it leaked. That is a different engineering problem from "search is bad," and it pointed us at the reranker instead of the retrieval arms.

The zero-result analysis was sharper still. One in five queries returned nothing to the user. But only 7.5% were true zeros where the index had no matches. The other five cases had results, and the reranker had demoted every one of them below the cutoff. A component that was supposed to improve precision was silently converting hits into empty pages.

And the confidence score the API attached to each answer turned out to mean very little. Answers marked "high confidence" had an average item grade of 1.67 out of 3. "Medium" averaged 1.24. Barely a gap, and both sat closer to "marginally related" than to "directly answers the need." The worst single case scored 0.14 on nDCG while reporting high confidence.

None of that was visible before the replay. All of it was visible the first afternoon.

## The part I have to be honest about

The relevance labels in that golden set were bootstrapped by a model, not graded by humans. Every label records who judged it, and today every one of those fields says the same model run.

We did that deliberately as a starting point, and we record the provenance so nobody mistakes it for ground truth. The plan is what the doc says: generate candidates from the live system, have humans grade them, merge the grades back. The cheapest source of new human labels is the review queue people are already working through. Every report-only run of the entity resolver writes a CSV that a person reviews, and those verdicts become labeled pairs. That resolver has ten labeled pairs today against a target of 150 to 200, and it is not allowed to auto-apply merges until its precision on that set clears 0.98, because an over-merge is unrecoverable and a missed merge is just future work.

I would rather publish those numbers than pretend the set is bigger than it is. A label with a recorded judge is worth more than a confident number with no provenance.

## The loop is never allowed to turn its own work green

Harvesting and replaying is the easy half. The harder half is what happens after an expert says the answer is wrong.

On the revenue system the workflow runs like this. A RevOps expert opens the evals page and records a failing grade on a question, with notes. The notes are the spec. An alert fires immediately. The open fails form a work queue, and each one gets classified from the notes: right answer but wrong shape, wrong definition, wrong question, or wrong tool behavior. Each of those maps to a different place in the code. The fix ships with tests, deploys, and the nightly run regenerates the answer.

Then an LLM judge reads the expert's complaint next to the new answer and decides whether the complaint looks addressed. It is deliberately narrow. It judges only the complaint, never whether the numbers are right, because separate deterministic machinery verifies arithmetic.

Here is the rule that matters: **the judge can only move the card to amber.** Never green. Only the expert recording a passing verification clears the flag. An expert's failing grade wins over a passing automated run, always, because the automated checks prove internal consistency and the expert grades business truth.

That is not a convention in a doc. The API refuses to accept judge rows through the human review endpoint. It refuses to edit them, and it refuses to delete them, because deleting a judge's fail would soften a red card to amber without anyone looking. Judge rows are excluded from the "verified" count. A test walks the whole sequence, expert fail then judge pass then judge fail then human pass, and asserts that only the last step produces a verified answer.

If you build a self-improving loop and let it grade itself, it drifts to green. Not through malice. Through the same mechanism that made my first harness pass: the thing producing the answer and the thing checking the answer share too much.

## Agreement on nothing is not agreement

The other lesson cost us four months.

The pipeline system reconciles its numbers nightly against the Data team's tables. For a long time one of those comparisons was green every night. Both sides were zero. The upstream table had stopped being populated, our copy died with it, and a sum over zero rows on both sides reconciled perfectly. A model summarizing the situation rationalized the empty number into "treat this as a minimum." Nobody looked, because nothing was red.

Three changes came out of that. Reconciliation checks now declare when they expect a nonzero value, and a matched pair of zeros fails instead of passing. A separate eval holds each source next to a number derived from somewhere else, on the principle that you cannot close more than you create, so a dead table disagrees with a live one instead of quietly agreeing with itself. And a stale reporting table is a hard failure, not a provenance note. A red eval is what makes someone look.

The same instinct drives the semantic layer underneath. There are 49 revenue measures and every one has exactly one definition, in one file. A test fails on known fork shapes, because a duplication sweep found the same taxonomy SQL hand-copied to five places. A pre-commit hook blocks any edit to a definition surface that does not also add a dated changelog entry, on the grounds that a definition change without a record is a silent fork. The golden runner answers every question twice, once through the engine and once through SQL written independently against the raw tables, and the two must agree within half a percent. A bug in the metrics layer disagrees with the independent path instead of confirming it.

## The number that actually matters

The engine passes about 90% of the golden questions. I do not think that number means much, and the doc that records it says why: the set was written from one expert's list, and the engine was built to that list.

The first person to drive it cold, in a live session, asked six questions. It could answer one of them that day.

That one-in-six is the real metric. The 90% measures how well we hit the target we drew. The cold session measures whether the target was in the right place. Both numbers go in the doc. Only one of them changes what we build next.

## What to do on Monday

1. **Find the channel.** Somewhere in your company, an expert answers questions with links or numbers. A support queue, a Slack channel, an RFP archive, a request form. That is your dataset.
2. **Write the cases down in a replayable shape.** Question, business context, what the expert picked. Keep the source. Record who judged every label, even if the first judge is a model.
3. **Replay against production and score the gap.** Recall tells you whether retrieval is the problem. Ranking metrics tell you whether ordering is. Calibration tells you whether your confidence score means anything. Expect the first run to hurt.
4. **Put a human gate on the write.** The loop can capture, replay, score, diagnose, and even fix. It cannot mark its own fix as passing. Enforce that in the API and lock it with a test, not a comment.
5. **Make agreement on zero a failure.** Any check that compares two sources needs to know when both being empty is the bug.

## Limits

This works when experts are already answering the questions somewhere you can read. If the expert knowledge lives only in people's heads, you still have to extract it, and the intake form trick only gets you the schema.

The replay depends on production data. Our retrieval eval cannot run against an empty local stack, which means it runs against the real deployment, which means it is slower and touchier than a unit test and gets skipped when the credentials are missing rather than faked.

Model-bootstrapped labels are a starting point, not a result. I have said that twice now because it is the part people skip.

And none of this measures whether the feature was worth building. It measures whether it does what the expert would have done. That is the question we could not answer before, and the one that turned out to be enough to ship on.
